# Habit Formation
In psychology, a habit is not just "something you do often." The definition
is a learned link between a context cue and a response. When the cue 
appears, it produces an impulse to act, with little need for conscious 
intention. Once formed, the behaviour can run with little deliberation and 
becomes partly independent of current motivation. This explains both why 
good habits are valuable (they keep going when motivation dips) and why 
they take patience to build (the link strengthens slowly).

1. Pick one specific behaviour and tie it to a stable cue. **(Strong, Good evidence)**
- **The context matter as much as the action**.

2. Anchor the new behaviour to an existing routine. **(Good evidence)**
- **To choose a cue you will meet every day, and then act on it every time**.

3. Write an if-then plan. **(Strong)**
- **If-then plan helps to shield from distractions and makes the responses more automatic**.

4. Start simple and make it enjoyable. **(Good evidence)**
- **Behaviour that you value and find it regarding are more likely to build strong habits**.
- **Morning practices generaly develop stronger habit strenght**.
- **Simple actions are more easily learned than more demanding ones, so keep first version small and increase it slowlly**.

5. Track the behaviour, ideally in writing. **(Strong)**
- **Setting goals are more effctive in motivating behaviour continuity**.
- **Making progress public is also very effective in progress**.

6. Plan for a long runway.
- **A habit takes between 60-154 days on average to start forming**.

7. Do not panic over a missed day, but get back on track quickly. **(Good evidence)**
- **One miss is harmless, but gaps that grow are a warning sign**.
- **Giving rewards when going back after missing an activity increase performance**.

8. Use life changes and fresh starts. **(Good evidence)**
- **Habits are cued by context. Using life changes and temporal landmarks gives more motivation to keep going**.

## Pitfalls
1. Relying on motivation and intentions alone.
- **Strong habits can override good intentions**.

2. Believing in a "21-day" magic number.
- **People who expect quick automaticity may conclude they have "failed" while the habit is still forming**.

3. Unrealistic expectations and the "false hope" cycle.
- **Unrealistic expectations about how fast, how much and how easily one can change can bring disappointment which increases desistence rates**.

4. Inconsistent context and timing.
- **If the context of where and when the behavior happens changes, no single cue gets linked**.
- **Being rigid about the place and time when a behavior can happen can backfire if it makes you skip it whenever you miss the window**.

5. Choosing behaviours that are too complex, unpleasant or imposed.
- **Pleasure and intrinsic motivation strengthened the link between repetition and habit, while merely knowing that a behaviour is useful did not**.

6. Expecting incentives or short programmes to create lasting habits on their own.
- **Short-term boosts often fade**.

7. Fighting old habits head-on in the old context.
- **Established habits persists even when motives change. Trying to override a well-cued habit through willpower, while leaving the cues unchanged, works against the mechanism**.

8. Relying on "willpower as a battery.
- **Design a behavior so it needs less effortfull self-control in the first place is a better-supported strategy**.

9. Being all-or-nothing after a lapse.
- **Treating one slip as total failure is therefore not justified by the data**.
